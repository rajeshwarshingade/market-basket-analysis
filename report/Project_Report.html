Abstract

Retail baskets contain patterns: some products are bought together far more often than chance would suggest. This project applies market basket analysis to the public Groceries dataset using the Apriori algorithm. The 9,835 baskets and 169 products are cleaned, one-hot encoded, and mined at 1% minimum support. Rules are generated with confidence >= 40% and lift > 1, producing 333 frequent itemsets and 62 rules. The strongest rule, {citrus fruit, root vegetables} -> {other vegetables}, has support 1.04%, confidence 58.62%, and lift 3.03. The results are visualised and translated into retail suggestions. The report also explains why association is not causation and why the dataset's age matters.

Keywords: market basket analysis, association rule mining, Apriori, support, confidence, lift

1. Introduction and Objectives

1.1 Data mining and market basket analysis

Data mining is the process of finding useful patterns in large datasets. In retail, one of the most direct patterns is co-occurrence: which products appear in the same basket. Market basket analysis, also called affinity analysis, studies these combinations.

If bread and butter appear together much more often than their individual popularity would predict, a store can place them near each other, bundle them, or recommend one when the other is selected. Association rule mining measures and ranks these relationships using support, confidence, and lift.

1.2 Problem statement

A store does not automatically know which products form meaningful combinations. The problem is to identify product sets that occur often enough to matter, generate directional rules, and separate strong associations from combinations that are common only because both products are popular.

1.3 Project objectives

  1. Load and clean a public grocery transaction dataset.
  2. Describe the data: transactions, products, and basket sizes.
  3. Identify the most frequently purchased products.
  4. Convert baskets into a one-hot encoded table.

  6. Generate rules using confidence and lift.
  7. Rank and display the most useful rules.

1.4 Scope and applications

The project is a descriptive co-occurrence analysis of one public dataset. It does not use prices, margins, quantities, customer IDs, dates, or store locations. It is an educational study, not a production recommendation system. Practical uses include product placement, meal bundles, targeted promotions, online recommendations, and testing store layouts.

2. Dataset and Methodology

2.1 Dataset source and description

The project uses the Groceries dataset, distributed with the R package arules and mirrored in the Machine Learning with R repository on GitHub (https://github.com/stedy/Machine-Learning-with-R-datasets). Each line of groceries.csv is one basket, with products separated by commas.

Computed description: 9,835 transactions, 169 distinct products, average basket size 4.41, median 3, maximum 32, and 2,159 single-item baskets (22.0%). The data are an older public snapshot, widely described as 1990s grocery transactions. The file has no date field, so the exact period cannot be verified. It also lacks prices, quantities, customer IDs, stores, and promotion flags.

2.2 Data cleaning and preprocessing

Each line is treated as one basket. The notebook strips whitespace, converts names to lowercase, removes empty and missing-like tokens (nan, none, null), removes duplicate products inside a basket, and drops empty baskets.

On the full file, these checks found no blank rows, no missing-like tokens, and no duplicate items; all 9,835 baskets were valid. The cleaning code is retained because real retail files are rarely tidy. Single-item baskets are kept: they are real purchases and affect support. They cannot form a pair themselves, but removing them would inflate the support of every pair.

2.3 Explanation of Apriori

Apriori finds frequent itemsets in stages. It counts single products, keeps those above minimum support, joins them into pairs, counts the pairs, and continues with larger sets. It uses the Apriori principle: if an itemset is infrequent, every larger itemset containing it must also be infrequent, so those candidates are skipped. This makes the search practical.

The minimum support is 1%, so an itemset must appear in at least ceil(0.01 x 9,835) = 99 baskets. This is a business choice: low enough to find useful pairs, high enough to avoid thousands of one-off combinations.

2.4 Support, confidence, and lift

For a rule A -> B:

  • Support = baskets containing both A and B / all baskets.
  • Confidence = baskets containing both / baskets containing A = P(B | A).
  • Lift = confidence / support(B) = P(B | A) / P(B).

Lift = 1 means no association beyond the baseline; lift > 1 means B is more likely when A is present; lift < 1 means they appear together less often than expected.

Worked example (computed): for root vegetables -> other vegetables, 1,072 baskets contain root vegetables, 1,903 contain other vegetables, and 466 contain both. Support = 466 / 9,835 = 4.74%; confidence = 466 / 1,072 = 43.47%; lift = 43.47% / 19.35% = 2.25. So 43.5% of root-vegetable baskets also contain other vegetables, 2.25 times the normal rate.

3. Results, Conclusion, and Future Scope

Computed findings: the numbers below come from running the notebook on the full 9,835-transaction dataset with 1% support, 40% confidence, and lift > 1. They are measured, not estimated.

3.1 Key findings

  • Whole milk is the most frequent product: 2,513 baskets (25.55%), then other vegetables (1,903; 19.35%), rolls/buns (1,809; 18.39%), soda (1,715; 17.44%), and yogurt (1,372; 13.95%).
  • Apriori found 333 frequent itemsets; filtering left 62 rules. The strongest rules are vegetable combinations.

3.4 Interpretation and retail use

  • Vegetables cluster. Group root vegetables, citrus fruit, tropical fruit, and onions near other vegetables; test a "vegetable curry pack".
  • Dairy supports milk. Curd, yogurt, butter, and sour cream have lift 1.8-2.3 with milk; use a shared chilled aisle or breakfast bundle. Because milk is already in one in four baskets, discount a complementary item rather than milk, and recommend other vegetables after root vegetables and citrus fruit (lift 3.03).

3.5 Conclusion

The project demonstrates a complete association rule mining workflow. Vegetables and dairy products co-occur in meaningful ways, and some rules are about three times stronger than baseline. However, broad categories hide SKU-level detail and association is not causation, so changes should be tested with a controlled experiment.

3.6 Limitations

  1. Age and missing context: no dates, prices, quantities, customer IDs, or promotions.
  2. Broad categories and thresholds: other vegetables is a category, not a specific product, and 1% support plus 40% confidence change which rules appear.
  3. Association is not causation: lift does not prove that buying A makes a customer buy B, and the results are not validated on a newer dataset.

3.7 Future improvements

Add prices and margins, use newer data, try FP-Growth, add stability checks, and test bundles or layouts with A/B experiments.
